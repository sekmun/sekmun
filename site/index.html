Sek-Mun Wong, Sydney

Build the loop, not just the code.

For most of my career, the expensive part of making software was people typing it. This year that stopped being true. Models now write competent code across a real stack, harnesses keep them working for hours at a time, and the cheapest of them now cost little enough to leave running overnight.

When implementation gets that cheap, the job moves. The work becomes designing the loop that code flows through: what goes in, what has to be proven before anything comes out, and who decides it ships. That's loop engineering. I think it's how most software will be built within a couple of years, and the teams who start learning it now will have a lead that's hard to close.

These are my notes from running a loop on a real production platform: why now, what to expect, and how I've set up each stage.

Why now

Three things arrived this year, and together they change the economics of building software.

The models became good enough to write production code you can actually keep. The harnesses became good enough to keep those models working, with tools, sub-agents and checkpoints, instead of answering one prompt at a time. And then the cost fell. On 9 September DeepSeek V4.1 Flash went live, and the token budgets and 5-hour usage windows that had been deciding when work could happen simply stopped mattering. A loop could finally run while I slept.

Here's what that looked like.

Sprint 57 was planned at 260 points and closed 744, with scope growing to 799 as fast as it could be specified. The total isn't the point; story points are a private measure, and nobody should compare them across teams. Look at the shape instead. The steepest part of the curve starts just after the cost dropped, and GitHub, which knows nothing about my sprints, lights up on exactly the same days.

The wider market is feeling the same shift. On OpenRouter, open-weight models have carried most of the token volume since April, peaking at 79% in early July and staying above 62% since. The money hasn't followed yet: closed frontier models still took 73% of spend in mid-September. That gap is the big question hanging over the frontier labs. If the work keeps moving to open models, how long can premium pricing hold?

Their own growth curve is starting to answer. Annualised figures hide it, so look at it quarter by quarter instead. On Epoch AI's numbers, OpenAI and Anthropic's combined run-rate revenue grew about 47% a quarter through 2025, surged to about 83% in the first quarter of 2026, then fell back to roughly 62% a quarter from April to July. The doubling time stretched from under three and a half months to over four.

That's still remarkable growth, but in this industry the gradient is what gets priced, and the gradient has turned. Valuations were set on the steep part of the curve. This week OpenAI cut the valuation it's asking new investors for by $150 billion, to $1.25 trillion, after pushback, and some of Anthropic's prospective investors are pressing its $2 trillion ask towards $1.5 trillion: a 25% haircut before it has even listed. Slowing growth at the frontier, with the token volume drifting to cheaper open models, is the clearest explanation I can see.

It helps to know where that growth came from. Last year a lot of heavy coding ran on flat subscriptions, and the heaviest users treated the $200-a-month plans as all-you-can-eat. Anthropic said as much when it added weekly limits in July 2025, citing subscribers running Claude Code “continuously in the background, 24/7” and others sharing and reselling accounts, and it pointed them to extra usage at API rates. OpenAI moved Codex onto a token-based rate card with purchasable credits in April 2026. My read is that much of this year's growth is that shift, from flat fees to metered, overage-driven tokens.

That works while the frontier is clearly better. The pushback this year is that open-weight models have become good enough for most of the work, at a fraction of the price. I think there's a limited window in which the market will keep paying the overage tax. After that, teams will either bring inference in-house, onto their own hardware or their own cloud, or move to open weights. It's one more reason to build your loop model-agnostic from the start: when the economics shift, you want to change a model, not your whole way of working.

What to expect

Nobody jumps straight to an unattended loop. In my experience it comes in stages, and each one moves the bottleneck somewhere new. Knowing where it has gone is most of the skill.

  1. AssistedThe model helps and you drive. It feels faster, but you're still the bottleneck, because every step waits for you.
  2. LoopedAgents implement inside gates you design: contracts go in, evidence comes out. Now the limit is tokens, and budgets and usage windows decide when work can happen.
  3. UnattendedOnce tokens are cheap enough, the loop runs overnight and through weekends. For me that moment was 9 September, in the middle of sprint 57.
  4. Specification-boundThen the backlog runs dry. The constraint moves upstream, to the thing that was always hardest: deciding precisely what to build. I think that's where the most interesting engineering is about to be.

How to build the loop

When implementation is cheap, everything around it has to get stronger. None of these stages are new; good teams already run all of them. The difference is that each one now has to hold up with nobody watching. This is how I've set each one up, and what I'd tell anyone starting out.

Plan Scrum, delivery management and BMAD

Agents don't remove the need for planning. They punish its absence.

I still run two-week sprints on a ClickUp board. Stories get points and epics don't, and scope that arrives mid-sprint is tracked separately, so when a sprint grows from 260 to 799 points you can watch it happen.

One habit worth adopting early: keep story points and model turns in separate books. Points are the scope you agreed and accepted; turns are how hard the models worked. Mix them up and you'll start measuring activity instead of outcomes.

BMAD is good for drafting a plan and then arguing with it. The rule I hold to is that a generated plan never outranks the code that has shipped or what's on the live tracker. A useful plan says plainly what exists, what's missing, and what still needs a person to decide.

Specify Contracts an agent can be held to

This is where the leverage is now. An agent builds exactly what you asked for, so the specification has to say what you actually mean.

I write each product decision as a contract: the invariants that must hold, how state moves, who owns which data, what happens when things fail, and what we're deliberately leaving out.

A change that crosses services becomes linked tickets, one per repository, each with its own branch and pull request, and schema ownership and compatibility are agreed before anyone touches code. Decisions like time windows, timezones and rule versions are stored as facts when they're made, so nobody has to reverse-engineer them later from whatever the settings say.

Build Multi-model, multi-language, isolated

Building is where most people start, and once the other stages are in place it's the one that matters least. A few choices still make a big difference.

Isolate everything. Each agent works in its own Git worktree against a throwaway database, so parallel agents can't trample each other and a bad run costs nothing to throw away. Checkpoints let a session pick up after an interruption without redoing finished work.

Choose the harness on evidence. I use omp.sh. Composio's August 2026 comparison ran the same model, Kimi K3, through eight harnesses, including Claude Code, Codex and OpenCode, on 25 real business-app tasks, scoring each by the final state of the apps. Oh My Pi, the project behind omp.sh, completed 22 of the 25, the best of the eight, at an estimated 52 US cents per successful task.

Choose models by role, not loyalty. Recent runs have used Claude Opus 5, GPT-6.1-Sol and DeepSeek's Flash and Pro models to implement, and Kimi K2.7-Code, through Kilo, to review. Treat cost as part of the design: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash is what turned my loop from a session into infrastructure. One orchestration archive from 6 to 21 October holds more than 6,800 assistant messages, before counting subagents.

If data retention is what's holding you back from DeepSeek, you don't have to send anything to DeepSeek's own API. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash is also available on Ollama's cloud subscription, and Ollama states that prompts and responses are never logged or trained on, and requires zero data retention from every provider it runs models with.

A polyglot stack works fine with agents, as long as each contract has one source. The platform runs Laravel with Octane and Vue, Python for the API and batch tiers, and TypeScript on Cloudflare Workers and Node.js. The database schema is owned by the Laravel migrations, and the TypeScript and Python services work from typed Drizzle and SQLAlchemy mirrors, so no service can quietly redefine it.

Verify Unit coverage, front-end tests, independent review

If you take one thing from this page, take this: tests are what let you trust code you didn't write.

Coverage is reported on every build and badged on each repository: 80.71% on the Laravel core and 95.39% on the Python services. I don't chase 100%. Coverage shows me where nobody has looked yet, and the tests show the code honours its contract.

Front-end behaviour is checked in a real browser against the real application, with viewports pinned and transitions controlled so the test can't invent layout bugs of its own. Results from local fault injection are kept apart from proof against live external services. Each repository adds formatting, types, schema compatibility and smoke tests on top. Review needs independence you can verify. The reviewer comes from a different model family to the author, and I check the routing metadata to confirm which model actually answered, because providers can fall back silently and leave a model marking its own homework. Treat findings as claims to check, not orders: a real correction earns a focused re-review, and cosmetic churn doesn't get to block a release.

Ship Infrastructure as code, GitHub Actions, gated releases

Put everything in code, the infrastructure included, and keep shipping as a series of separate decisions.

The whole platform is infrastructure as code, and every build, test, release and deploy runs in GitHub Actions. That's what lets an agent work on the infrastructure as safely as it works on a feature: same pull requests, same checks.

Merging, releasing, deploying and switching a feature on are four different decisions. Additive schema changes go out before the services that depend on them, releases pass through UAT before production, source versions are pinned and rollback evidence is kept. Container images are promoted exactly as tested, and Workers that haven't changed aren't redeployed. A feature can be switched on as its own rolling configuration change, without rebuilding accepted code or surprising existing customers.

A small story shows why this matters. In a recent acceptance test for a scheduled workflow, the business outcome was committed first and delivery recorded separately for each destination. One destination accepted; the external service deliberately rejected the other. The stored outcome stayed put, the interface showed a partial delivery, and the one-off job didn't spawn a successor. When you can't tell whether a send went through, say so. Never report it as a success, and never blindly retry it.

Run Edge architecture, telemetry, security, scale

Design for the edge, and instrument the loop as carefully as the product.

Signed events land on Cloudflare's edge. Each signature is checked and a provisional deduplication claim is taken, coordinated per workspace by Durable Objects; the claim only becomes final once the event is safely queued, and it's released if queuing fails. Live processing and archiving have separate retry and dead-letter paths, so trouble with the archive never eats into the live budget. A Python batch tier compacts the raw archive in R2 into Iceberg tables partitioned by day and tenant, reusing embeddings and caching configuration so that model cost is controlled by design rather than by a billing alert.

After every deploy I check what's actually running: its version, health and applied configuration. A green workflow only proves the pipeline finished. On the loop side, every agent session is archived with the model that really served each response, so I can always see who wrote and who reviewed anything that reached production.

Security lives in the boundaries. Requests are authorised per workspace with PostgreSQL row-level security underneath, tested under a role that can't bypass it, because a tenant-isolation test running as a superuser proves nothing. The privileged ingest and batch services sit behind their own documented trust boundary. Alongside that: raw-body HMAC verification with bounded replay windows, single-use OAuth state bound to the user, encrypted credentials kept out of normal serialisation, explicit channel scopes, and audited operator sessions that expire. Access is always all, assigned or none, never an empty list that can switch filtering off by accident.

The platform is designed for 1,000 workspaces and around 1.5 million messages a day, and I treat that as a target until production proves it. Performance work starts from query plans and realistic fixtures, not hunches. On a 200,000-row fixture, partial and expression indexes took one hot lookup from 35.7 ms to 0.10 ms, once both sides of an OR had been made indexable.
